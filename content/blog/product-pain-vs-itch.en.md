The easiest thing for an indie developer to be fooled by is one sentence:

"This idea is awesome."

This sentence sounds great but is often worthless. Many people praising you doesn't mean they actually need it. They might just be polite, might just think the direction sounds good, might just want to encourage you.

You're talking with great enthusiasm, and they don't want to pour cold water on you, so they casually toss you a line:

"Sure, that's pretty valuable."

And then you get carried away.

You think you've validated the demand, but you've actually only validated that the other person is a decent human being.

A user's verbal "awesome" at most proves your idea doesn't sound stupid.

It can't prove the user has a need, can't prove the user is willing to migrate, can't prove the user is willing to use it daily, and even less can it prove the user is willing to pay.

## Verbal praise hurts the most

The most hurtful thing in product research is verbal praise.

- "Tell me when it launches."
- "I'll definitely use it once you build it."
- "This direction has a lot of market."
- "A lot of people around me need this."

These all sound like demand, but most are just social lubricant. They make you comfortable, make you confident, make you mistakenly think you've touched the user's pain point. When you actually build it and send the link over, the other person might not even bother to register.

Only then do you realize that their "awesome" back then was just another way of saying "good luck."

## There's only one hard standard for judging demand

See what the user is willing to give up.

Money is the hardest signal. Willingness to pay means the problem really stings them.

Money isn't the only cost. Time, data migration, actively trying it out, referring colleagues, tolerating a rough version — these all count as costs too.

But if a user isn't willing to pay any cost and only wants to praise you a couple of times, it's most likely just an itch point.

**Pain points make people act. Itch points only make people polite.**

## Don't mistake friends' encouragement for a market

Many indie developers, when building a product, like to start by talking to friends about the idea.

A friend says it's good, so you start building. A netizen says they want to use it, so you start building. Someone in the comments says "count me in," and you think the market has arrived.

The real market is in payment records, in trial bookings, in the user being willing to send you their current ugly Excel sheet, in the user being willing to tell you how much money they waste on this problem every month, in the user being willing to tolerate bugs for a half-finished product.

- A person willing to use ten patches to work around a problem is in pain.
- A person willing to pay monthly for a substitute is in pain.
- A person willing to spend half an hour explaining their workflow to you is in pain.
- A person who only says "this idea is good" and then takes no follow-up action — don't take it too seriously.

There's a very accurate judgment in *置身钉内*:

To judge whether a user accepts a product, you can't just listen to whether they say "good" or "bad" — you have to look at what they're willing to give up for the product. They can pay money, pay time, pay attention, pay migration cost, pay organizational authorization, or pay psychological safety.

Because indie developers don't have a big company's trial-and-error budget.

You can't burn half a year on "everyone says it's good." You need to know as early as possible whether this demand is actually a business.

## Ask the question more realistically

Don't ask: "What do you think of this product?"

That question too easily gets you nonsense.

Ask:

- "How do you solve this problem now?"
- "Have you ever paid for this problem?"
- "Would you pay an early-bird price right now?"
- "Would you send me a screenshot of your current workflow?"
- "If I make a really ugly MVP, would you try it next week?"

After asking, watch the actions.

A person in real pain will give you details. They'll talk about the current trouble, the alternatives, the budget, what they've tried, and why they're annoyed.

A person in fake pain will only give you opinions. They'll say "this direction is good," then turn around and keep scrolling their phone.

**Opinions are worthless. Behavior is valuable. Paying is the most valuable.**

Many products fail fundamentally because they mistake "someone praised it" for "someone will buy it."

These two things are far apart. The person praising you is consuming your expression; the person buying from you is solving their own problem.

You can listen to praise, but don't use praise to make decisions.

You can collect positive feedback, but don't treat positive feedback as orders.

You can believe in the direction, but in the end you have to return to one question:

**What is this person willing to give up for it?**

## "Looks valuable" often means no one is in a hurry to buy

The thing indie developers fear most is building a product that "looks valuable."

"Looks valuable" often means everyone can understand it, everyone can praise it a couple of times, but no one is in a hurry to buy it.

A truly good early product is usually not that grand. It might only solve one lousy little thing for a small group of people, but that lousy thing is annoying enough, frequent enough, and wasteful enough. The moment you bring it out, the other person won't say "this direction is good" — they'll directly ask:

How much?

When can I use it?

Can I try it today?

That's the good signal.

When users say "awesome," don't get carried away.

Make them pay, make them try, make them migrate, make them give up a little real cost.

Once the cost appears, the demand reveals itself.

And the difference between pain points and itch points comes out too.
